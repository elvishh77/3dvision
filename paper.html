How it works

From hand trajectory to video

We follow the Motion Prompting recipe (arXiv 2412.02700), which conditions a video model on point tracks through a ControlNet. Our base model, Wan2.1-Fun-1.3B-InP, is a diffusion transformer rather than a U-Net, so the "ControlNet" is a trainable copy of its first 8 transformer blocks. Their outputs pass through zero-initialised projections and are added to the frozen model's hidden states, so training starts exactly at the base model and the adapter only learns what the hands add.

Training

Flow-matching loss on 2,728 HOT3D clips, 10k steps, lr 1e-4. Text and hand conditioning are each dropped 10% of the time.

Two loss variants

Plain loss vs. plain loss plus extra terms on the hand region (weight 1) and the contact region (weight 2). Both train side by side.

Cost

About 18 hours per run on 3 × 48 GB GPUs (~55 GPU-hours); the base model stays frozen.

The conditioning volume

What the adapter sees, per latent frame

Motion Prompting writes a random embedding for each point track at its pixel location. We do the same for the 42 hand keypoints (21 per hand), and add channels that a point track can't express: the hand's surface, which of its skin is in contact, and how far it is from the camera. Each latent frame covers 4 video frames, so the volume holds 4 sub-frame slots of 64 channels.

Scores on held-out clips

Hand error and PCK compare hands detected in the generated video (HaMeR) with the conditioning trajectory. Contact F1 uses HACO on hands that touch something. Task accuracy is a Qwen2.5-VL checklist of the prompt's outcome.

How we evaluate

Four questions, each with a validated metric

Does the video follow the hands?

Detect hands in every generated frame with HaMeR and measure the 2D keypoint error against the conditioning trajectory, capped so a missed hand costs a fixed penalty. Also PCK at 25 px and a jitter ratio relative to the real clip.

Does it touch the right things?

HACO estimates per-vertex contact from the generated frames; we score F1 against the conditioning contact on active hands. A VLM checks that idle hands aren't gripping anything.

Does the outcome match the prompt?

Qwen2.5-VL-7B answers yes/no questions derived from the prompt (was the object lifted, where did it end up) at keyframes; we report balanced accuracy over the set.

Is the metric trustworthy?

Every metric was first run on real clips paired with deliberately wrong trajectories (shuffled, reversed, frozen, left/right swapped). A metric is used only if it clearly prefers the true pairing.

Questions

Design choices, briefly
Why Wan2.1-Fun-1.3B-InP?
It's conditioned on a first frame and text natively and is small enough to train an adapter in about a day on a few 48 GB GPUs.
Why 512 × 512 and 49 frames?
Aria frames are square, and 49 frames compress to 13 latent frames, which keeps a training step at about 6 seconds on 3 GPUs.
Why an adapter instead of fine-tuning the whole model?
The frozen model keeps its video prior, the adapter starts as an exact no-op, and training fits in memory without checkpointing the base model.
Why describe the outcome rather than the first frame?
The first frame is already an input. An outcome prompt adds information the model doesn't have: what should have happened by the end.
What about robot hands?
Phase 2. The data format already reserves a slot for dexterous robot hands mapped to the same conditioning volume.
